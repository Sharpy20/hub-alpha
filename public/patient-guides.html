How Mental Health Medicines Work

Patient information – understanding medication in a non-blaming way

You can read this if you are curious about how mental health medicines work and why they are sometimes prescribed. If you have been offered medication, or are already taking it, it is important to know: accepting support is not weakness, and you have every right to ask questions. Having a mental health difficulty does not mean there is something wrong with you.

Many people worry that mental health medicines are unnatural or harmful, or that they contain chemicals their body does not need. That concern is understandable. This guide attempts to explain, in clear and honest terms, how these medicines actually work and why they are prescribed.

Your brain already uses chemical messengers

Your brain cells communicate with each other using natural chemical messengers. The main ones involved in mental health include serotonin (mood stability, emotional processing, sleep), dopamine (motivation, reward, pleasure, confidence), noradrenaline (alertness, energy, focus), and GABA (the brain's main calming chemical, which controls anxiety and helps you rest). Everyone has these chemicals. Your brain produces and manages them all the time.

When someone is unwell, regulation becomes harder

When a person is under stress, mentally unwell, traumatised, physically unwell, or overtired, the brain's messaging systems can become unsettled. This does not mean something is broken. It means the brain is finding it harder to regulate itself. Messages between brain cells may become:

  • too strong
  • too weak
  • too fast
  • too slow
  • or poorly timed

This can affect how a person feels, thinks, reacts, or sleeps.

What mental health medicines actually do

Mental health medicines usually do not add new chemicals that your body does not recognise. Instead, they work with the chemical systems your brain already has. For example, some medicines help serotonin stay active for longer. Others strengthen GABA's calming effect. Some reduce overactive dopamine signalling. The aim is to support regulation, not to override your brain.

  • increase calming signals
  • reduce overactive or alerting signals
  • help signals last a little longer
  • block certain signals from being too strong
  • help the brain respond more evenly to messages it is already receiving

What medication does not do

Mental health medicines do not change who you are, erase your personality, control your thoughts, or replace your brain. The aim is not to sedate or numb you. The aim is to support your brain while it is under strain, so it has a better chance to recover and function more smoothly.

When medication does not go smoothly

It is important to be honest: medication does not work for everyone, and it does not always go smoothly. Side effects are real and can be significant. Some people try several medications before finding one that helps. Some people find that no medication helps enough to be worth the downsides.

These are valid experiences. If medication is making things worse, causing distressing side effects, or not helping, your voice matters and you deserve to be heard. Staff should take your concerns seriously and explain the options available to you.

Medication is a tool, not a punishment. What matters is that conversations about it involve you, your experience, and honest information about both the potential benefits and the real risks. Even when the clinical situation is complex, your perspective should always be part of the discussion.

A note on the world we live in

Mental health is not only shaped by brain chemistry. Poverty, housing insecurity, discrimination, abuse, isolation, and inequality all contribute significantly to mental distress. Medication can support the brain's regulation, but it cannot fix the circumstances that may be contributing to how someone feels. Good care recognises both – the biological and the social – and does not expect medication alone to do everything.

A final reassurance

If medication is offered, it is not because you are weak. It is because your brain is under pressure, and sometimes it needs extra support to regain balance.

⚛

We would not ask someone with Type 2 diabetes to manage their blood sugar through willpower alone – we support the body’s insulin regulation with medication. We would not tell someone with an underactive thyroid to simply try harder – we give the body the thyroxine it is struggling to produce. Mental health medication works on the same principle: supporting a system in the body that needs help regulating. The brain is an organ. It can struggle, and it can be supported.

Nobody chooses to have a mental health difficulty any more than someone chooses a thyroid condition or high blood pressure. Accepting support is not failure. Your voice matters in these conversations. You deserve clear answers about what you are taking and why, and your concerns should always be listened to.

✎

Sleep and Mental Health

A practical guide for patients and staff to use together

You can read this if you are curious about why sleep changes during mental health difficulties and what might help. If sleep is hard for you right now, that is very common when the brain is under strain, and it is not your fault.

Why sleep is often affected

Sleep can be disrupted by anxiety, distress, trauma responses, changes in routine, ward environments, or medication changes. Poor sleep can then worsen mood, anxiety, concentration, and coping. This can become a cycle.

How sleep normally happens

Your brain uses natural chemical signals to help you slow down mentally, relax physically, and feel sleepy at the right time. During the day, alerting signals are stronger. At night, calming signals normally take over. Mental illness can disrupt this shift, making it harder to switch off.

Where medication fits

Sleep medication is sometimes used when sleep problems are severe, distress is high, or other approaches have not helped enough. It works by supporting the brain’s existing calming systems and is usually reviewed regularly.

Sleep medicines explained

Sleeping tablets – zopiclone, zolpidem

These boost the effect of GABA – a calming brain chemical that acts like a brake pedal, reducing brain activity so it is easier to fall asleep. They work quickly but the brain can adapt fast. Usually short-term only, reviewed regularly. Not recommended where there is a history of substance misuse or significant falls risk.

Anti-anxiety medicines that also cause sleepiness – temazepam, diazepam

These also act on GABA, more broadly. Anxiety reduces, muscles relax, sleepiness follows. Helpful when anxiety is stopping sleep, but the brain can become reliant on them. Used carefully and for short periods.

Melatonin

Melatonin is a hormone your body already makes. It acts like a time signal, telling the brain it is night time. Rather than forcing sleep it helps reset the sleep-wake rhythm. Very low risk of dependence. Works best when taken consistently and at the same time.

Antidepressants used for sleep – mirtazapine, trazodone

At lower doses these block histamine (a wake-promoting chemical) and affect serotonin. The brain becomes less alert, drowsiness increases, and mood or anxiety may also improve. Less addictive than sleeping tablets. The sleep effect can wear off over time.

Antihistamines – promethazine

These block histamine causing drowsiness. Can cause dry mouth, blurred vision, confusion. Not usually first-choice and not suitable for everyone, especially older adults.

Practical ways to support sleep

Small, realistic steps can help alongside medication. Not everything will work for everyone.

Sleep hygiene

  • keeping roughly the same sleep and wake times
  • reducing caffeine later in the day
  • avoiding bright screens before sleep
  • keeping the sleep area calm and comfortable
  • using the bed mainly for sleep where possible – on a ward this is not always realistic, but worth considering after discharge

Some sleep hygiene advice is easier to follow at home than on a ward, where noise, observations, and a shared environment can disrupt sleep in ways outside your control. That is a real limitation of the ward environment, not a personal failing.

Living in Survival Mode

Understanding chronic stress, hyperarousal, and nervous system overload

Many people describe feeling constantly on edge, unable to relax, or always on alert even when nothing bad is happening. This is not weakness. It is often a sign that the body’s stress system has been working too hard for too long.

Your body’s alarm system

The human body has a built-in alarm system designed to keep us safe – often called the fight-or-flight response. When the brain senses danger, it releases stress chemicals such as adrenaline and cortisol. These are normal chemicals that everyone’s body produces. This system is very useful in short bursts. It is not designed to stay switched on all the time.

What happens in the brain during fight or flight

When the alarm fires, the brain redirects its resources. Blood and energy shift away from the parts of the brain responsible for rational thinking, language, and planning, and toward the parts that handle survival. This is why, in moments of acute stress, you may:

  • repeat the same words or phrases without meaning to – the speech centres in the brain are partially offline
  • struggle to find words or explain what is wrong
  • say things you do not mean, or say nothing at all
  • find it impossible to think clearly, make decisions, or process what someone is telling you
  • experience tunnel vision or tunnel hearing, where only part of the world gets through
  • feel time slow down or speed up

None of this is a choice. The brain is prioritising survival over communication.

Four threat responses

The survival system does not just produce fight or flight. There are four common responses, and most people shift between them depending on the situation:

  • Fight – moving toward the threat. This can look like anger, shouting, pushing back, or verbal aggression
  • Flight – moving away from the threat. This can look like pacing, trying to leave, avoidance, or hiding
  • Freeze – going still while still highly alert inside. This can look like going blank, being unable to speak or move, or seeming to zone out
  • Fawn – trying to appease the threat. This can look like agreeing to everything, people-pleasing, telling staff what you think they want to hear, or being excessively compliant

Fawn is particularly important to understand. On a ward, it can look like genuine engagement or agreement when in reality the person is terrified and doing whatever feels safest. Apparent compliance is not always real agreement.

The window of tolerance

There is a zone where the brain can think, feel, and communicate at the same time. Psychologists call this the window of tolerance. Above the window is hyperarousal – anxiety, panic, rage, restlessness. Below it is hypoarousal – numbness, shutdown, collapse, disconnection. When someone is outside their window, they cannot process complex information, make good decisions, or have productive conversations.

Stress, trauma, poor sleep, and feeling unsafe all narrow the window. This means the brain tips into survival mode more easily and stays there longer.

How long the chemicals take to clear

Adrenaline takes roughly 20 to 30 minutes to start settling after the threat has passed. Cortisol can linger for hours. This means that even after the situation is over, the body may still be running as if the danger is present. Attempting a meaningful conversation, making important decisions, or processing complex information during this window is usually futile and may re-escalate things.

The most helpful thing during this time is calm, predictable presence. Not questions. Not explanations. Not problem-solving. Just safety, and time.

When the alarm does not switch off – hyperarousal

Hyperarousal is the medical term for when the body’s alarm system stays active even when there is no immediate danger. Senses remain heightened, thoughts race, emotions feel overwhelming, and the body struggles to relax. This is commonly seen in anxiety and trauma-related conditions but can happen to anyone under ongoing stress.

Chronic stress

When stressors do not stop and the body remains on high alert for weeks or months, high levels of adrenaline and cortisol can affect sleep, blood pressure, digestion, immune function, mood, and energy. People often feel worn down but still unable to rest.

Survival mode

In survival mode, the brain is constantly scanning for threat, prioritising getting through the day over rest or enjoyment. It may include fight, flight, freeze, or people-pleasing responses. It develops because the body believes it must stay alert to stay safe.

A dysregulated nervous system

When the alert system dominates for too long, the nervous system becomes dysregulated. Instead of returning to calm after stress, the body may stay constantly tense, or swing between agitation and emotional shutdown. This is a state of strain, not weakness.

When the stressors are still present

Much of what helps with survival mode involves creating safety and reducing threat. But it is worth naming honestly: if the circumstances causing the stress have not changed – if the housing is still unsafe, the relationship still difficult, the financial pressure still real – recovery looks different. Managing a sensitised nervous system in an ongoing difficult situation is not the same as recovering from one that has passed. Both are valid, and both need different kinds of support.

Common signs

  • hypervigilance – constantly on guard or alert for danger
  • sleep problems even when exhausted
  • exhaustion or burnout
  • irritability, panic, or emotional outbursts
  • sensory sensitivity to noise, light, or touch
  • muscle tension – clenched jaw, tight shoulders, headaches

Practical coping – sending signals of safety

Recovery is not about forcing relaxation. It is about gently teaching the nervous system that it is safe to stand down. Small, repeatable actions are more effective than big changes.

Understanding Anxiety and Panic

Why the body can feel unsafe and what can help

You can read this if you are curious about why anxiety and panic happen and what is going on in the body when they do. If these experiences are happening to you, it is important to know: you matter, and help is available. Anxiety is not a character flaw. It is often the brain’s threat system working overtime.

What anxiety is

Anxiety is the feeling of threat or danger even when there is no immediate danger in front of you. The body may react as if it needs to protect you. The brain is doing its job – it is just doing it too much, too often, or in response to things that are not truly dangerous.

What panic is

Panic is a sudden surge of intense fear with strong physical symptoms. It can feel like something terrible is happening. Panic attacks are frightening, but they are not the same as being in danger. The feelings, though very real, are caused by the alarm system misfiring, not by actual threat.

What is happening in the body

The brain releases adrenaline and cortisol – chemicals your body already produces as part of its threat response system. Adrenaline causes the immediate physical symptoms: racing heart, fast breathing, sweating. Cortisol keeps the body in alert mode for longer. This can cause:

  • racing heart, chest tightness, or shaking
  • fast breathing or feeling short of breath
  • dizziness or feeling unreal or detached
  • sweating, nausea, or an urgent need to escape

These sensations are the body preparing to react to danger. They are uncomfortable, but they are not harmful.

Why anxiety can keep repeating

After a frightening episode, the brain may start scanning for the same sensations again. Worry about anxiety can increase anxiety. Avoiding situations can make them feel more threatening over time. This is very common and it is not your fault.

What helps in the moment

When anxiety is high, simple grounding is often more helpful than long conversations.

Managing Overwhelm and Emotional Flooding

When feelings arrive too fast or too strongly

Sometimes emotions feel so intense that thinking clearly becomes very difficult. This is common during severe stress, trauma responses, psychosis, or heightened anxiety. It is not weakness. It is a nervous system under pressure.

What overwhelm is

Overwhelm happens when the nervous system is overloaded. The brain focuses on safety and survival, and the thinking part of the brain can go offline temporarily. In those moments, reasoning and problem-solving are genuinely harder – not because something is wrong with you, but because of how the brain prioritises under threat.

Common signs

  • feeling flooded or completely out of control
  • sudden anger, tears, panic, or complete emotional shutdown
  • difficulty speaking or finding words
  • urge to escape, hide, lash out, or push people away
  • feeling very young, or disconnected from yourself

What happens in the brain during overwhelm

When the brain is flooded, the parts responsible for speech, reasoning, and decision-making become less active. This is a physical process, not a choice. You may find yourself repeating the same words, unable to explain what is wrong, or going completely silent. The brain is redirecting its resources toward survival. It takes roughly 20 to 30 minutes for the stress chemicals to begin clearing, and during that time, complex conversation or decision-making is genuinely harder.

Why talking through it does not always help in the moment

When the brain is in overwhelm, the thinking, reasoning part has less influence over the reacting part. This is why talking through problems in the moment of flooding is often not helpful – the brain is not in a state where it can process that kind of support until the system settles.

What helps before overwhelm escalates

  • notice early signs and tell staff before escalation
  • reduce stimulation – noise, light, people, demands
  • slow breathing to signal safety to the nervous system
  • grounding with the senses
  • movement such as walking or stretching if safe

What helps in the moment of overwhelm

  • one person, speaking calmly and slowly
  • simple, short sentences, no demands
  • quiet space and time to settle
  • physical safety without crowding
  • writing things down rather than speaking if easier

Understanding Low Mood and Depression

Why motivation and energy can disappear and what can help

You can read this if you are curious about why low mood and depression affect energy, motivation, and daily life. If these experiences are happening to you, it is important to know: you matter, and help is available. Depression is not laziness, weakness, or self-indulgence.

What depression can feel like

  • low mood or a persistent feeling of emptiness
  • loss of interest or enjoyment in things that used to matter
  • feeling slowed down physically or mentally
  • exhaustion that does not improve with sleep
  • sleep changes – too much or too little
  • appetite changes
  • difficulty concentrating or making decisions
  • self-critical thoughts or a sense of hopelessness

What is happening in the brain

Your brain already uses chemical messengers to manage mood, motivation, reward, and energy. The key ones here are serotonin (mood stability and emotional processing), dopamine (motivation, reward, and the ability to feel pleasure), and noradrenaline (energy and alertness). When someone has been under prolonged stress, loss, or trauma, these systems can become depleted or dysregulated. Dopamine-driven reward and motivation pathways can become less active, which is why things that used to bring pleasure or interest no longer do. This is not a choice and it is not failure.

Why trying harder does not always work

Depression reduces the very resources needed to recover from it. Motivation is affected by the same chemical systems that depression disrupts. Telling someone to just try without addressing the underlying state is like asking an injured person to run. The difficulty is real, not imagined.

Small steps matter – when they are possible

Recovery often begins with very small, realistic steps. Even minor activity or connection can gently stimulate the brain’s reward and motivation systems.

When depression is very severe, even small steps may feel completely out of reach. If that is where you are, this is not a further failure – it is the illness at its most acute. The steps below become more accessible as treatment begins to take effect. The aim at the most severe point is simply to be safe and supported, not to achieve.

  • gentle routine, even if minimal
  • short bursts of activity followed by rest
  • connection with staff or supportive people, even brief
  • sleep support and basic nutrition
  • time outside or near natural light where possible
  • noticing very small moments of ease or interest

Where medication fits

Some medication supports mood regulation by working with chemical systems your brain already has. SSRIs, for example, help serotonin stay active in the brain for longer. Others work on noradrenaline or dopamine pathways. The aim is to support the systems that have become depleted, not to create artificial feelings. Medication may help reduce the intensity of low mood, improve sleep, reduce anxiety, and create space for recovery. These medicines often take time to have their full effect. This is because the brain needs time to adjust – not because the medication is not working.

A final reassurance

Depression often tells people that nothing will help and that things will always feel this way. That is the illness, not the reality. With the right combination of support, improvement is possible.

It is also worth naming that depression does not always arise from brain chemistry alone. Poverty, isolation, discrimination, loss, and the cumulative weight of difficult circumstances are real contributors. Medication and therapy can help significantly, but good care also asks what in the person’s situation needs to change – not only what in their brain chemistry needs adjusting.

✎

It may help to tell staff:

  • what times of day are hardest for me
  • what small goals feel even slightly possible
  • what helps me feel less alone
  • what makes low mood worse – for example feeling criticised, rushed, or overwhelmed
  • what I need to hear – or not hear – from staff

How Trauma Can Affect the Brain and Body

A trauma-informed guide to understanding reactions

You can read this if you are curious about how trauma affects the brain and body. If you have experienced trauma, it is important to know: many trauma responses are the brain and body doing exactly what they learned to do in order to survive. Understanding this can help make sense of experiences that might otherwise feel frightening or confusing.

What trauma is

Trauma refers to experiences that overwhelmed the brain’s ability to cope. This can include single events or ongoing situations. What makes something traumatic is not only what happened, but how the brain processed it at the time.

Trauma responses are protective

After trauma, the brain’s threat system can become more sensitive. The brain may keep looking for danger even when you are now safe. This is the brain doing its job – protecting you – based on what it learned.

Common trauma responses

  • hyperarousal – feeling on edge, jumpy, unable to relax
  • intrusive memories, flashbacks, or nightmares
  • avoiding people, places, or thoughts that are reminders
  • freeze or shutdown – feeling numb, disconnected, or unable to act
  • strong emotional reactions that make complete sense in context
  • difficulty trusting people or feeling safe

Why the past can feel present

The brain stores traumatic memories differently from ordinary memories. They can be stored with all the sensory information from the original event. When something triggers one of these cues, the brain may react as if the original event is happening again. This is not a choice. It is how trauma memories are stored.

What is happening in the brain

Trauma causes changes in how stress chemicals such as adrenaline and cortisol are released – chemicals your brain already produces. After trauma, the threshold for releasing them can become lower, meaning the alarm fires more easily. The brain also holds body memories – physical sensations and responses that can be triggered by reminders, even without conscious awareness.

What helps

  • predictable routines and consistent, reliable staff
  • clear information and no sudden changes where possible
  • grounding and breathing techniques to manage the present
  • safe relationships without pressure to disclose
  • psychological therapy when the person is ready and stable
  • medication to support sleep, reduce threat sensitivity, or stabilise mood

Understanding Dissociation and Shutdown

When the mind or body disconnects to protect you

Some people experience feeling numb, unreal, far away, or as if they are not fully present. This can be dissociation. It is often a protective response – the brain’s way of managing overwhelming experience.

Depersonalisation and derealisation

Dissociation is an umbrella term covering different experiences. Two of the most common are:

Depersonalisation – feeling detached from your own body or self. You may feel as if you are watching yourself from outside, that your body does not belong to you, or that your thoughts and feelings are not quite real. You know who you are, but it does not feel like it.

Derealisation – feeling that the world around you is unreal, dreamlike, flat, or distant. Objects, people, and surroundings may look different – too vivid or too muted, too near or too far.

Some people experience both. Some only one. Others experience different things entirely – losing track of time, feeling that different parts of themselves are separate, or emotional numbness.

Why it happens

When the brain believes that fighting or fleeing will not work, it may shift into a freeze or shutdown response. This reduces emotional pain in the short term. Dissociation often develops after repeated trauma or overwhelming experiences. It is a protective mechanism, not a failure.

What can trigger dissociation

  • reminders of past trauma
  • feeling overwhelmed, unsafe, or threatened
  • high levels of stress or exhaustion
  • sensory overload
  • certain environments or interactions

What helps – grounding back to the present

Understanding Psychosis

What may be happening in your mind and body

You can read this if you are curious about what psychosis is and what may be happening in the brain during these experiences. If these experiences are happening to you, it is important to know: you matter, and help is available. Having these experiences does not mean there is something wrong with you. Psychosis is a word used to describe experiences that can be frightening, confusing, or overwhelming. These experiences are real to the person having them, even if others do not see or hear the same things.

This guide is probably most useful at a time of relative stability, or to help make sense of experiences after an acute episode. If you are currently in the middle of an acute psychotic episode, the ideas here may not land easily – and that is expected, not a problem with you.

Psychosis looks different for everyone

There is no single correct way for psychosis to look or feel. These experiences can be very hard to describe, especially when they are new or distressing.

  • hearing voices that others do not hear
  • seeing things others do not see
  • feeling watched, unsafe, or extremely suspicious
  • strongly held beliefs that feel completely real
  • thoughts feeling louder, faster, or harder to organise
  • difficulty telling what feels real from what does not

Why these experiences feel so real

Your brain’s job is to take in information, decide what is important, and decide what might be threatening. In psychosis, this system can become overwhelmed or overactive. The brain may assign too much meaning to neutral events, internal thoughts may feel like they come from outside, and threat detection may stay switched on. Because this happens automatically and below conscious control, the experiences feel completely real. They are not chosen.

Stress, sleep, and the brain

Psychotic experiences often become more intense when the brain is under strain – high stress, poor sleep, loss of routine or safety, feeling overwhelmed. Stress raises chemicals such as adrenaline and cortisol – normal chemicals that everyone’s body produces. When they stay high for too long, they can disrupt how the brain processes information.

How medication can help

Medication used for psychosis works with systems your brain already uses – primarily dopamine pathways. In psychosis, dopamine signalling can become overactive, particularly in the brain's threat and meaning-detection circuits. Antipsychotic medication helps bring this back into a more regulated range. It does not put alien or poisonous chemicals into your body. It may reduce over-amplification of signals, help the brain filter information more evenly, lower background threat levels, and support sleep and routine.

⚛

Taking medication for psychosis is no different in principle to taking medication for any other condition where the body’s regulatory systems need support. We do not ask someone with epilepsy to manage seizures through determination, or someone with heart failure to strengthen their heart through positive thinking. The brain is part of the body, and it can need support in exactly the same way.

Medication supports regulation. It does not remove your identity, personality, or ability to think for yourself.

Recovery is possible – and means different things

Many people who experience psychosis recover fully or learn ways to manage their experiences and live well. A diagnosis does not define your future. Recovery often takes time, and setbacks are a normal part of the process.

For some people, particularly those who have had multiple episodes, recovery does not mean experiences disappear completely. Recovery can mean learning to relate to those experiences differently, reducing their distress and impact, and building a life that feels meaningful alongside them. That is a valid and real form of recovery.

✎

Care Planning Tips

  • something feels different to how it normally does for me
  • my thoughts or perceptions do not feel right or usual
  • things feel more intense or frightening than usual
  • I am finding it hard to know what is real
  • noise and busy spaces make things worse
  • when I do not sleep, everything gets more intense
  • calm, clear information and routine help me

Why self-harm can happen

People self-harm for different reasons. Common experiences include:

  • overwhelming emotions that feel impossible to manage
  • feeling numb and wanting to feel something
  • a need for control when life feels completely out of control
  • intense self-critical thoughts or feelings of self-punishment
  • a way to communicate pain that words cannot express
  • relief from dissociation – a way to feel present again

What is happening in the brain and body

Your brain uses chemical messengers to manage pain, emotion, and stress. Self-harm can trigger the release of natural pain-relieving chemicals, which can create temporary relief. Over time, the pattern can reinforce itself and may increase shame, which in turn increases distress.

Self-harm is a coping strategy that can be replaced

The aim is not to take self-harm away without offering something in its place. Recovery involves understanding the function it serves and building other ways to manage the same intensity of distress. This takes time and support. It is not about willpower.

What helps

  • telling someone early when urges rise – before they become overwhelming
  • grounding and breathing to reduce intensity in the moment
  • safe distraction and connection with someone trusted
  • writing or drawing to externalise the internal experience
  • psychological support to understand patterns and triggers

Understanding Suicidal Thoughts

When life feels too painful or too much

You can read this if you are having thoughts about not wanting to be here, or if you want to understand what can happen during extreme distress. If these thoughts are happening to you, it is important to know: you matter. These thoughts are often a sign of unbearable pain, not a sign that you truly want to die.

Thoughts, urges, and plans are different

Suicidal thoughts exist on a spectrum. Some people have passing thoughts without any intent to act. Some people feel urges. Some people feel at serious risk. Staff will take all of these seriously and will support you without judgement. Having these thoughts does not make you dangerous or bad. It makes you someone in significant pain.

Why these thoughts can happen

When the brain is overwhelmed, it can enter what might be called escape thinking. The mind starts searching for a way out of unbearable pain. Suicidal thoughts are often not a desire to end life as much as a desperate need for the pain to stop. This is the brain under extreme strain, not a sign of who you are.

Why it is important to tell someone – and what it may mean

Suicidal thoughts tend to be most dangerous when they are kept secret. It is worth being honest about what disclosure may mean: telling staff is likely to change some things about your immediate care. Observations may increase, planned leave may be paused, or your care team may review your plan. This is not punishment – it is staff responding because they want to keep you safe.

What it should not mean is being judged, dismissed, or treated as a problem. Staff will try to involve you in any decisions about your care wherever possible.

If telling staff feels frightening because of previous experiences – being treated differently, feeling less trusted, or care becoming more restrictive – that fear is understandable and worth naming. You can tell staff what you are worried about at the same time as telling them how you are feeling. That helps them respond in a way that feels safer.

What helps

  • telling staff or someone you trust as early as possible when thoughts increase
  • reducing isolation – being with people even if not talking
  • focusing on getting through the next hour rather than thinking further ahead
  • grounding and calming skills when distress spikes
  • treating sleep problems and physical health alongside emotional support

Riding it out

When suicidal thoughts are at their most intense, the aim is not to solve everything. It is to get through the next hour. These are not techniques that fix the problem. They are ways to stay here long enough for the intensity to pass.

  • tell someone – a nurse, a friend, anyone. Saying it out loud changes its power
  • stay around people, even if you do not talk
  • focus only on the next hour, not tomorrow or next week
  • cold water on your face or hands – this activates a physical calming reflex
  • hold something with weight or texture – a cold drink, a blanket, your own hands
  • write down what you are feeling, even if it does not make sense
  • remind yourself: this feeling is temporary, even when it does not feel that way

In a crisis

If you feel unable to keep yourself safe, tell a member of staff immediately. In the community, contact NHS 111, your local crisis team, or Samaritans on 116 123 (free, 24 hours). In an emergency, call 999 or go to A and E.

  • what situations make the thoughts more intense
  • what helps me feel safer and more connected
  • how I prefer staff to support me when I feel at risk
  • whether there is anything staff are doing that is making things harder

Understanding Eating Difficulties and Eating Disorders

Why food, control, and safety can become linked

You can read this if you are curious about eating difficulties and why food, control, and safety can become linked. If this is part of your experience, it is important to know: you matter, and help is available. Eating difficulties are not about vanity or willpower. They are often about safety, control, emotion, and coping.

How eating difficulties can develop

Eating difficulties take many different forms. Restriction, bingeing, purging, rituals around food, avoidance of certain textures or types of food, and a complex relationship between eating and emotion can all be part of the picture. This guide applies across the range – including anorexia, bulimia, binge eating disorder, ARFID (avoidant or restrictive food intake disorder), or those who have never received a formal diagnosis but know that eating is a significant source of difficulty.

These behaviours often temporarily reduce distress. Over time they can become a cycle that is very hard to step out of alone. This is not a lifestyle choice and it is not a phase. It is often a deeply entrenched coping mechanism that developed for understandable reasons.

Ambivalence about recovery is normal

One thing this guide wants to name directly: not everyone reading it wants to recover, or is sure they do. Part of you may want things to change. Another part may not be ready, or may find the eating disorder so interwoven with your identity that the idea of recovery feels like a threat rather than a relief.

This ambivalence is not a failure of motivation. It is one of the most well-recognised features of eating disorders. The eating disorder has often been serving important functions for a long time. Letting go of it is not simple.

If you are not sure you want to recover right now, you do not need to pretend otherwise. Honesty about where you actually are is more useful than performing readiness. Staff who understand eating difficulties will not be shocked by ambivalence. They will work with you from wherever you are.

What the brain and body do under restriction

When the body is under-fuelled, the brain prioritises survival. Thinking can become rigid, repetitive, or obsessive. Mood and sleep often worsen. These cognitive changes are a direct result of under-nourishment, not a personality trait. This is biology, not choice. Recovery from the psychological aspects is often not fully possible until there is some degree of physical nourishment.

Why insight can fluctuate

People with eating disorders may find that their understanding of the situation changes depending on how physically and emotionally depleted they are. This is not dishonesty or manipulation. It is the impact of the eating disorder on the brain’s ability to process information clearly.

What helps

  • regular, consistent support from staff and specialists
  • regular nutrition with support – not as punishment
  • psychological support to understand triggers, functions, and beliefs
  • addressing anxiety and distress directly alongside food-related work
  • kind, non-judgemental language about food and the body

Understanding Emotional Intensity and Relationship Patterns

Often associated with a diagnosis of personality disorder

This guide is not about labelling you or blaming you. The word ‘personality disorder’ is a clinical term that many people find unhelpful or distressing. What it often describes is a nervous system that became very finely tuned to threat, and coping patterns that developed to manage very difficult circumstances.

What has happened – not what is wrong

The patterns described by this diagnosis rarely develop in a vacuum. They are usually connected to experiences of trauma, neglect, loss, invalidation, or repeated situations where the person’s needs were not met or their experiences were not believed. What has happened to you shapes how your nervous system responds to the world. That is not a character defect. It is an adaptation.

Why emotions can feel so intense

Some people have a naturally more sensitive threat system – partly biological, partly shaped by experience. Emotions can arrive very quickly, feel much bigger, and take much longer to settle than for other people. This is not drama or manipulation. It is a nervous system responding at a higher level of intensity.

Common experiences

  • intense fear of abandonment or rejection, even in stable situations
  • rapid mood changes that can feel overwhelming
  • feelings of emptiness or uncertainty about who you are
  • strong reactions to what might seem like small things to others
  • impulsive actions during periods of high distress
  • difficulty in relationships – between intense closeness and intense hurt
  • dissociation or self-harm as ways of managing overwhelm

A note on experiences with services

It would not be honest to write this guide without acknowledging that many people who have received this diagnosis have had poor or harmful experiences with mental health services. Dismissal, being labelled as difficult or manipulative, having distress attributed to personality rather than taken seriously – these are experiences many people have had, and they are not acceptable.

This guide describes what good care should look like. If your experience has not matched that, your anger or mistrust about it is entirely understandable. That history does not define what is possible now, but it deserves to be acknowledged.

A note on language

Words like ‘manipulative’, ‘attention-seeking’, or ‘not engaging’ cause real harm and reinforce shame. The patterns that get labelled this way are almost always attempts to communicate pain or maintain safety. What looks like difficult behaviour is usually a person in extreme distress, doing what their nervous system has learned to do.

What helps

  • consistent, predictable support from staff – same people, same approach
  • clear communication and consistent expectations that feel fair rather than punitive
  • skills to manage distress and regulate emotion, for example DBT approaches
  • feeling genuinely heard without being judged or dismissed
  • psychological therapy that addresses the experiences underneath the patterns

Recovery Is Not Linear

Why progress includes setbacks, and what helps

You can read this if you are going through a setback or wondering why progress does not feel like a straight line. If that is where you are, it is important to know: you matter, and setbacks are a normal part of recovery, not a sign of failure.

Why setbacks happen

  • stress reactivates the threat system and stress chemicals rise again
  • poor sleep reduces the brain’s ability to regulate emotion
  • big changes or transitions can overwhelm coping
  • trauma reminders or anniversaries can trigger old patterns
  • illness, medication changes, or life events can affect stability

Setbacks are not starting over

Each time you recover from a setback, the brain builds more experience of regulating. Even if it does not feel that way, progress includes going through difficult periods and coming out the other side. Shame about setbacks often makes them last longer. Self-compassion is not weakness – it is a recovery skill.

What helps during a setback

  • noticing early warning signs before things escalate
  • using coping skills earlier rather than waiting
  • asking for support sooner rather than later
  • keeping routines as simple and consistent as possible
  • reducing demands and expectations during hard periods
  • remembering that this state is temporary, even when it feels permanent

What recovery actually looks like

  • episodes becoming shorter or less intense
  • recovering more quickly after setbacks
  • having a larger range of coping skills
  • feeling able to ask for help sooner
  • having periods of genuine wellbeing in between difficulties

A note about stopping treatment

One of the most common reasons for setback is stopping medication or reducing support too quickly. This often happens during a period of feeling well – which is a natural time to question whether treatment is still needed. It is always worth discussing changes to treatment with staff before making them. The brain may need more time to stabilise than the absence of symptoms suggests.

Understanding Autism

A guide for autistic people and those who support them on the ward

You can read this if you are curious about how autism affects someone's experience, especially on an inpatient ward. If you are autistic, it is important to know: autism is not a disease, a disorder of thought, or a problem to be fixed. It is a different neurological profile – a different way the brain is wired to process information, communicate, and experience the world. Many autistic people find inpatient mental health environments particularly difficult. This guide attempts to explain why, and what can help.

What autism is

Autism is a lifelong neurological difference. It affects how a person processes sensory information, communicates, relates to others, and manages change and routine. It exists on a wide spectrum – no two autistic people are the same. Autism is not caused by upbringing, vaccines, or trauma. It is largely genetic. The brain of an autistic person is structured and wired differently, not defectively.

Sensory processing

Many autistic people experience the world through heightened or differently calibrated senses. What feels ordinary to one person can feel genuinely painful or overwhelming to an autistic person. On a ward this might include:

  • fluorescent lighting that causes physical discomfort
  • background noise that cannot be tuned out
  • the feel of certain clothing, bedding, or materials being intolerable
  • smells from food, cleaning products, or other people
  • being touched without warning feeling threatening or distressing

These are not preferences in the everyday sense. They are the result of a nervous system processing sensory input at a different intensity. They are real and they are valid.

Communication differences

  • needing more time to process what has been said before responding
  • finding eye contact uncomfortable, distracting, or painful
  • preferring direct, literal language over suggestions or implications
  • finding it hard to identify or describe internal emotional states
  • communicating more easily through writing or structured formats
  • finding small talk difficult or exhausting

If an autistic person seems quiet, blunt, or unresponsive, this is usually not rudeness. It is a communication style that may need a different approach from staff.

Masking and its cost

Many autistic people have spent years learning to appear non-autistic in social situations. This is called masking. It involves suppressing natural responses, copying others’ behaviour, and performing social norms that do not come naturally. Masking is exhausting. By the end of the day, or during a crisis, the ability to mask often collapses entirely. A mental health crisis may be partly the result of the cumulative exhaustion of masking. It is not a sign that the autism was not real.

Meltdowns and shutdowns

A meltdown involves the nervous system discharging overwhelm outwardly – intense distress, crying, shouting, or loss of control of responses. It is not a tantrum. A shutdown involves withdrawing inward – becoming quiet, unresponsive, or unable to communicate. Both are signs of overload, not behaviour problems. Responding with calm, reduced stimulation, and minimal demands is usually the most effective approach.

Why ward environments are particularly difficult

  • unpredictable routine and frequent changes
  • high levels of sensory stimulation with little control
  • multiple unfamiliar people and inconsistent communication
  • implicit social expectations that are difficult to read
  • lack of private, quiet space
  • intrusive observations and interruptions to routine

Autism and mental health

Autistic people experience higher rates of anxiety, depression, and burnout. Autistic burnout – profound exhaustion after prolonged overload – can look very similar to depression. When an autistic person is in hospital, it is important that their autism is recognised and accommodated, not treated as secondary to the mental health presentation.

Where medication fits

There is no medication that treats autism itself, and no medication is needed – autism is not an illness. However, medication is sometimes used to support co-occurring difficulties such as anxiety, depression, ADHD, or sleep disruption.

⚛

Just as someone with a neurological difference affecting their vision uses glasses not to fix their eyes but to function more comfortably in a world designed for different vision, medication for co-occurring conditions supports functioning without changing who the person is.

Understanding ADHD

What is happening in the brain and how it affects daily life

You can read this if you are curious about ADHD and what is actually happening in the brain. If you have ADHD, it is important to know: this is not laziness, bad parenting, or lack of effort. It is a recognised neurological difference. You have probably been trying harder than most people around you for a long time, with a brain that is not wired for the environments you are expected to function in.

What ADHD is

ADHD does not mean a person cannot pay attention – it means attention is regulated differently. Many people with ADHD can focus intensely on things that interest or engage them. The difficulty is with tasks that are low-stimulation, repetitive, or do not generate immediate reward. ADHD presents in different ways:

  • inattentive presentation – difficulty sustaining attention, easily distracted, forgetful, loses things
  • hyperactive or impulsive presentation – physical restlessness, difficulty waiting, speaks before thinking
  • combined presentation – features of both

ADHD is often not diagnosed until adulthood, particularly in women, where the presentation is more likely to be inattentive and internal rather than hyperactive and visible.

What is happening in the brain

Your brain already uses chemical messengers to manage attention, motivation, and impulse control. In ADHD, the systems that use dopamine and noradrenaline do not work in quite the same way. These are chemicals your brain already produces. In ADHD, they may be released less consistently, making it harder to sustain attention, start or complete tasks, and regulate impulses – not because of a lack of willpower, but because of how the brain’s chemistry is organised.

Executive function and ADHD

Executive function refers to the brain’s ability to plan, prioritise, start tasks, manage time, organise, and regulate emotion. ADHD significantly affects this.

  • knowing what needs to be done but being unable to start
  • underestimating how long tasks take
  • difficulty organising thoughts or belongings
  • moving between tasks before finishing them
  • struggling to hold information in mind while doing something else

Emotional dysregulation

Many people with ADHD experience emotions very intensely and find them hard to regulate. Frustration, excitement, and disappointment can arrive quickly and feel overwhelming. This is sometimes called rejection sensitive dysphoria – an intense emotional response to perceived criticism, rejection, or failure. This can look like overreaction from the outside. From the inside, the emotional experience is entirely real and proportionate to how it feels.

ADHD medication and hospital admission

It is worth knowing that ADHD medication is sometimes not continued during an inpatient admission. This can cause real and significant deterioration in concentration, emotional regulation, and daily functioning. If you usually take ADHD medication, tell staff clearly and early in your admission. Your voice matters here. Ask for it to be continued, and if the decision is made not to continue it, you deserve a clear explanation of why.

Where medication fits

ADHD medication works by supporting the brain’s dopamine and noradrenaline systems – chemicals your brain already uses. It does not change personality or create focus artificially. For many people, it allows the brain to do what it was already trying to do more reliably.

⚛

A hearing aid does not give someone hearing they would not otherwise have. It supports the auditory system to function in a world designed for a particular range of hearing. ADHD medication works on the same principle – supporting the attention and regulation systems to function in a world designed for a particular style of brain. It is not cheating. It is appropriate support for a genuine difference.

✎

Understanding Bipolar Disorder

What is happening in the brain and how episodes can be managed

You can read this if you are curious about bipolar disorder and what happens during mood episodes. If this is part of your experience, it is important to know: you matter, and help is available. Bipolar disorder is not a character weakness, an excuse, or something you brought on yourself. It is also not simply being very happy sometimes and very sad other times. The mood states involved are much more intense, much longer lasting, and much more disruptive than everyday emotional variation.

What bipolar disorder is

Bipolar disorder involves episodes of significantly elevated or irritable mood, sometimes with psychotic features, alternating with episodes of depression. Between episodes, many people function well. There are different presentations:

  • Bipolar I – involves full manic episodes which may require hospital admission, and depressive episodes
  • Bipolar II – involves hypomanic episodes (less severe than full mania) and significant depressive episodes
  • Cyclothymia – a milder pattern of mood instability over a prolonged period

What is happening in the brain

Your brain already uses chemical messengers to regulate mood, energy, sleep, and arousal. In bipolar disorder, the systems that manage these chemicals – particularly involving serotonin, dopamine, and noradrenaline – do not cycle in a stable way. This is not a choice. The brain’s regulatory systems for mood are genuinely dysregulated.

⚛

Just as the thyroid can overproduce or underproduce thyroid hormone in a way that is entirely outside conscious control, the brain’s mood-regulating chemistry can shift in ways that are not chosen or willed.

Mania and hypomania – from the inside

Early stages of mania often feel very positive – increased energy, reduced need for sleep, heightened confidence, racing ideas. This can make it hard to recognise as a problem. As mania escalates, it may involve:

  • significantly reduced need for sleep without feeling tired
  • very rapid or pressured speech
  • racing thoughts that are hard to slow down
  • impulsive or risky decisions
  • grandiose beliefs about abilities or significance
  • paranoia, irritability, or suspiciousness
  • in severe mania, psychotic features such as hearing voices

During a manic episode, the ability to accurately assess one’s own state is genuinely impaired. This is not stubbornness – it is the condition itself.

The grief of losing the highs

One thing that is rarely acknowledged openly: many people with bipolar disorder mourn the loss of their elevated states. Hypomania in particular can feel like the best version of yourself – more creative, more confident, more energetic, more connected, more alive. Being told that the aim of treatment is to reduce that state can feel like a loss, not a gain.

This grief is real and it deserves to be spoken about. If you feel conflicted about treatment because you value some of what mania or hypomania brings, that is worth discussing honestly with your care team. It is a very common experience, and pretending otherwise does not help anyone.

Triggers and the role of sleep

Sleep disruption is one of the most significant triggers for mood episodes in bipolar disorder. Even a single night of poor sleep can be a warning sign or a trigger.

Where medication fits

Mood stabilisers – such as lithium or valproate – help regulate the cycling of mood chemistry. They are often described as turning down the volume on both ends of the mood range. Antipsychotic medications are sometimes used to manage manic episodes. Antidepressants are used carefully in bipolar disorder and usually alongside a mood stabiliser.

⚛

Taking medication for bipolar disorder is no different in principle to taking medication for any other condition where the body’s chemistry needs external support to stay in a functional range. Someone with Type 1 diabetes does not produce enough insulin and takes it externally. Someone with bipolar disorder has mood-regulating chemistry that cycles outside a functional range and takes medication to support stability. Neither is a weakness. Both are sensible medical management.

✎

  • what my typical sleep pattern is and what changes mean

  • what I want staff to do if I seem to be becoming unwell
  • who in my life knows my baseline and can offer useful information

Understanding OCD

What is happening in the brain and why the cycle keeps repeating

You can read this if you are curious about OCD and why the cycle keeps repeating. If these experiences are happening to you, it is important to know: you matter, and help is available. OCD is not about being tidy or fussy. The thoughts that occur in OCD feel completely at odds with who you are. Having these thoughts does not make you a bad person.

What OCD actually is

OCD involves two connected elements that form a cycle:

  • Obsessions – intrusive, unwanted, distressing thoughts, images, or urges that are hard to dismiss
  • Compulsions – actions, rituals, or mental processes performed to reduce the anxiety caused by the obsession

The compulsion provides temporary relief. But it also teaches the brain that the obsession was a real threat worth responding to, which makes the obsession return – often stronger.

Common themes in OCD

  • contamination and fear of illness or spreading harm to others
  • checking – doors, appliances, safety – driven by fear of catastrophic outcomes
  • harm obsessions – intrusive thoughts about hurting oneself or others, despite having no desire to do so
  • religious or moral obsessions – fear of being sinful or morally bad
  • symmetry and exactness – things must be arranged just right
  • relationship obsessions – repeated doubt about feelings or the rightness of a relationship

Intrusive thoughts are not desires

Having an intrusive thought is not the same as wanting to act on it. Most people have occasional intrusive thoughts that pass quickly. In OCD, the brain flags them as significant and dangerous, and the person becomes caught in attempting to prove they are not true. If you are frightened and distressed by a thought, that is evidence that it is OCD. People who genuinely intend harm are not usually horrified by their thoughts.

What is happening in the brain

In OCD, a part of the brain involved in error detection and threat appraisal becomes overactive. It generates a signal that something is wrong even when nothing is. This is sometimes described as a faulty alarm. Serotonin and glutamate – chemicals your brain already produces – are involved in this system and are the target of the most effective medications for OCD.

Why compulsions make it worse

Each time a compulsion is performed in response to an obsession, the brain learns that the obsession was a genuine threat. This is why the treatment that works best – Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) – involves sitting with the anxiety of the obsession without performing the compulsion. It teaches the brain that the alarm was false. This is incredibly hard to do and needs proper support.

OCD on the ward – a note on reassurance

Staff responses that inadvertently provide reassurance – answering the same question repeatedly, confirming safety, agreeing the person would never harm anyone – can unintentionally maintain the OCD cycle. The most helpful approach is compassionate non-reassurance – acknowledging that the anxiety is real and distressing, without reinforcing the belief that the obsession requires a response.

This is worth explaining clearly: non-reassurance does not mean staff will be cold or withhold support. It means they will not answer the specific question OCD is asking – the one that temporarily reduces anxiety but keeps the cycle going. Warmth, presence, and practical support are not the same as OCD reassurance.

✎

Care Planning Tips

It can help to tell staff:

  • what my OCD focuses on and what my compulsions are – staff need to understand these to avoid accidentally reinforcing them
  • what triggers my OCD on the ward
  • what helps me manage the anxiety without giving in to compulsions
  • whether I am working on ERP and what stage I am at
  • what I need from staff – I may need compassionate non-reassurance rather than comfort

Grief and Bereavement

Understanding loss and what happens in the brain and body

You can read this if you have experienced a significant loss and want to understand what is happening. If grief is overwhelming you right now, it is important to know: you matter, and grief is not a mental illness. But it can be so intense that it feels like one, and it can trigger or worsen mental health difficulties in people who are already vulnerable.

What grief is

Grief is the response to loss. It is most often associated with bereavement – the death of someone important – but grief can follow any significant loss:

  • the death of a person or a close animal companion
  • the end of a relationship
  • loss of health, ability, or a part of your identity
  • loss of a role – such as being a parent, a worker, or a carer
  • loss of a home, a community, or a way of life
  • pregnancy loss or fertility loss
  • loss of the future you expected

Grief can also be disenfranchised – meaning the loss is not socially recognised or validated. The grief of losing a pet, a friendship, or an abusive parent can be just as real as any other, and just as complex.

What grief can feel like

  • intense sadness, longing, or yearning
  • anger – at the person who died, at life, at yourself
  • guilt and regret – things said or unsaid, done or undone
  • shock, numbness, or disbelief, even when the loss was expected
  • relief – particularly after a long illness – which can bring its own guilt
  • anxiety and fear about the future
  • loneliness even when surrounded by people
  • moments of feeling fine followed by waves of intense grief

What is happening in the brain

Grief activates many of the same brain systems as physical pain. When someone important dies or a significant loss occurs, the brain’s attachment systems – deeply connected to reward and motivation pathways – are disrupted. The brain has learned to expect that person or thing to be there. The absence triggers a prolonged adjustment response. This is why grief can feel physical as well as emotional – exhaustion, chest pain, inability to eat or sleep – these are real physical responses.

What helps

  • having space and time to grieve without pressure to feel better
  • being listened to without being told how to feel or how long it should take
  • connection with people who knew and can talk about the person lost
  • gentle routine and self-care as a foundation, not a distraction
  • professional bereavement support when grief is severe or complicated
  • allowing both the waves of grief and the moments of relief without guilt

What does not help

  • being told to move on, or that the person would want you to be happy
  • having the loss minimised or compared to other losses
  • being expected to function normally too soon
  • being told there are stages and you should be past a particular one

Where medication fits

Medication is not usually the first response to grief, because grief is a natural process. However, when grief has triggered or worsened severe depression, severe anxiety, or significant sleep disruption, medication can support the brain’s regulatory systems to function well enough to cope with daily life.

⚛

Medication in this context is like supporting the body with pain relief during recovery from a physical illness – not fixing the underlying process, but making it possible to get through.

A final reassurance

Grief is the price of love and connection. The depth of the pain often reflects the depth of the bond. There is no timeline for grief, and there is no correct way to experience it. If your grief has brought you to a point where you need extra support, that is not a failure. It is evidence of how real and important the loss was.

✎

  • how long ago the loss happened and how it has changed over time
  • what I find hardest – particular times of day, anniversaries, or reminders
  • whether I want to talk about the person or thing I have lost, or prefer not to
  • what has helped and what has not helped from others so far

Substance Use and Mental Health

Understanding dual diagnosis without blame

You can read this if you are curious about the link between substance use and mental health. If this is part of your experience, it is important to know: you matter, and help is available. Using substances when you are struggling mentally is often an understandable response to pain, not a moral failure.

What dual diagnosis means

Dual diagnosis refers to when a person experiences both a mental health difficulty and a substance use problem at the same time. The two are closely connected and often make each other worse. Both are treatable, and both deserve proper attention.

Self-medication – not weakness

Many people use substances because they work, at least in the short term. Alcohol reduces anxiety. Cannabis can quieten a racing mind. Stimulants can lift depression temporarily. Opiates can numb emotional pain that feels unmanageable. This is called self-medication. It makes sense as a coping strategy, particularly for people who did not have access to proper mental health support or whose pain was dismissed.

The problem is not that it was chosen. The problem is that it works less and less over time, requires more to achieve the same effect, and begins to cause its own significant harm.

What substances do in the brain

Most substances work by altering chemical systems your brain already uses:

  • alcohol enhances GABA – the brain’s calming chemical – reducing anxiety and inhibition
  • cannabis affects endocannabinoid receptors involved in mood, memory, and pain regulation
  • opiates activate the brain’s natural painkilling and reward pathways
  • stimulants flood dopamine and noradrenaline systems involved in mood, energy, and motivation

None of these are alien chemicals. They are working on systems your brain already has – but doing so in ways that are much more intense and immediate than the brain’s natural regulation, which is why dependence develops.

Why stopping is so hard

When substances are used regularly, the brain adjusts – it compensates by producing less of its own equivalent chemical. This is called neuroadaptation. When the substance is stopped, the brain is temporarily short of the chemical balance it had adapted to. This produces withdrawal. During withdrawal, mental health symptoms typically worsen significantly. Anxiety, depression, paranoia, and sleep disruption can all increase sharply. Stopping without support is difficult, and proper medical management of withdrawal is important.

Shame is the biggest obstacle

Shame is one of the most damaging aspects of dual diagnosis. Many people with substance use problems have been told they are weak, selfish, or hopeless. This shame makes it harder to seek help, harder to be honest, and harder to engage with support.

It is also worth acknowledging something that does not get said enough: for many people, being honest about substance use with services has not felt safe, because it has not always been safe. Disclosure has sometimes led to being discharged, judged, or refused treatment. If that has been your experience, your wariness about being honest now is entirely understandable.

What we can say is that good care does not work that way. Honesty about substance use should lead to better, more joined-up support – not punishment. If you are worried about what being honest might mean for your care, it is worth asking that question directly before deciding whether or not to disclose.

A final reassurance

Dual diagnosis is common, treatable, and not a sign of a failed person. The circumstances that contribute to both substance use and mental health difficulties – poverty, trauma, adverse childhood experiences, housing insecurity, discrimination, bereavement, isolation – are not personal failings. They are things that happen to people, and they have real effects. Recovery is more possible when those circumstances are also taken seriously.

Recovery from both is possible. Many people do it every day.

✎

  • what the substances were doing for me – what they helped with
  • how long this has been happening
  • whether I have tried to stop before and what happened
  • what mental health symptoms feel most connected to my substance use
  • what I am most worried about in relation to stopping or cutting down

Understanding Anger

A non-judgemental guide to what anger is and where it comes from

You can read this if you want to understand anger better and where yours comes from. If anger is something you struggle with, it is important to know: anger is a natural human emotion. It is not a disorder, a failure of character, or something that needs to be eliminated. It is a signal, and it often carries important information about pain, injustice, boundaries, or fear.

What anger is

Anger is an emotion associated with perceived threat, injustice, frustration, or violation of something important. It is part of the body’s protective system. The aim of this guide is not to take anger away. It is to help you understand where yours comes from, what it is doing, and how to work with it rather than against yourself.

What is happening in the brain

Anger involves the release of adrenaline and cortisol – chemicals your brain already produces – along with activation of the parts of the brain responsible for threat detection. When the anger response fires, blood pressure rises, muscles tense, and the thinking part of the brain has less influence over the reacting part. This is why it can feel so difficult to think clearly when very angry. The brain is in its protective mode, not its problem-solving mode.

Why clear thinking shuts down

During intense anger, the brain's language and reasoning centres become less active. This is why people may repeat themselves, say things they do not mean, or struggle to articulate what is actually wrong. The brain is in its protective mode, not its communication mode. This typically takes 20 to 30 minutes to settle. Attempting to reason with someone – or with yourself – during this window is usually counterproductive.

Underneath anger

Anger is often described as a secondary emotion, meaning it frequently sits on top of something else:

  • fear – anger can be a response to feeling unsafe, threatened, or out of control
  • shame – anger can emerge as a defence when shame feels unbearable
  • grief and loss – anger is one of the most common responses to loss
  • powerlessness – anger often arises when someone feels they have no voice
  • hurt – anger can manage the pain of feeling rejected or let down
  • injustice – anger in response to unfairness is often valid and important information

Sometimes the anger is right

Hospital admission can itself be a significant trigger for anger. Being in an unfamiliar environment, having limited control over daily life, encountering unexpected rule changes, waiting with no information, feeling dismissed or not believed – all of these are objectively frustrating.

It is important to say this clearly: sometimes the anger is right. Some things that happen on a ward are genuinely unfair. Rights are not always upheld. Feeling angry about those things is not a symptom – it is a reasonable response to a real situation. Acknowledging that does not mean that any expression of anger is appropriate. It means that the goal is to understand anger, not to manage it away.

Anger, trauma, and mental health

Anger is a particularly common feature in the context of trauma, PTSD, bipolar disorder, personality disorder presentations, psychosis, and substance withdrawal. When the threat system is sensitised by past experience, anger responses can be triggered more easily. In the context of trauma, anger is often a protective response that developed for very good reasons.

What helps – before anger escalates

Anger is easier to work with early. Noticing the early signs and responding to them is much more effective than trying to manage full escalation.

  • rising physical tension – jaw, shoulders, chest, hands
  • thoughts becoming faster, more negative, or more absolute
  • increased irritability or sensitivity to small frustrations
  • feeling crowded, trapped, or disrespected

What helps in the moment

  • removing yourself from the situation if possible
  • movement – walking, stretching, physical release of tension
  • naming the emotion internally: I am feeling angry
  • telling a trusted person how you are feeling before it builds

Mindfulness and Being Present

Practical ways to slow down and notice what is happening right now

You can read this if you are curious about mindfulness and whether it might help you. Mindfulness is not about emptying your mind, sitting cross-legged, or achieving inner peace. It is simply the practice of noticing what is happening right now, without judging it. That is it.

What mindfulness actually is

Mindfulness means paying attention to the present moment on purpose. Not the past, not the future, not the running commentary in your head about what you should be doing. Just what is happening right now. When thoughts come – and they will – you notice them and let them pass without grabbing hold. Like watching clouds drift across a sky. You are not trying to stop them. You are just not chasing them.

Why it can help

When the brain is stuck in threat mode, it spends most of its time scanning for danger, replaying past events, or rehearsing worst-case futures. Mindfulness interrupts that loop. It gently activates the parasympathetic nervous system – the part of the brain responsible for calming down. Over time, this can widen the window of tolerance and make it easier to stay grounded during stress.

Body scan

This can be done lying down, sitting, or even standing. Start at your feet and slowly move your attention upward through each part of your body. Notice what is there without trying to change it. Tension in the jaw? Notice it. Heaviness in the chest? Notice it. The point is awareness, not fixing.

  • feet – notice the contact with the floor or bed
  • legs – notice any tension or heaviness
  • stomach – notice your breathing moving here
  • chest – notice the rise and fall
  • shoulders – notice if they are lifted or dropped
  • face – notice the jaw, the forehead, the eyes

If your mind wanders, that is not failure. That is the exercise working. You noticed it wandered. Bring it back gently.

Mindful breathing

This is different from box breathing or controlled breathing. You are not trying to change your breath. You are just noticing it. Breathe however your body wants to breathe, and pay attention to the sensation of air moving in and out. Notice where you feel it most – nostrils, chest, stomach. When thoughts pull you away, come back to the breath. No judgement. Just return.

Mindful walking

Walk slowly, indoors or outside. Notice each foot as it lifts, moves, and lands. Feel the floor or ground. Notice the air on your skin. Count steps if it helps. This works well on a ward because it uses movement, which is often easier than sitting still when the mind is busy.

Leaves on a stream

Close your eyes or soften your gaze. Imagine a gentle stream. When a thought arrives, place it on a leaf and watch it float past. Do not push it away. Do not follow it downstream. Just let it go. If a thought sticks, notice that too. It is all part of it.

The RAIN technique

When a difficult feeling arrives, try this sequence:

  • Recognise – name what is happening. "I am feeling anxious."
  • Allow – let it be there without fighting it. "This is here right now."
  • Investigate – notice where you feel it in your body. What does it actually feel like?
  • Nurture – offer yourself the kindness you would offer a friend in the same situation.

When mindfulness is not helpful

This needs saying clearly: mindfulness is not for everyone, and it is not appropriate in every situation. If you are in the middle of a flashback, severe dissociation, or a manic episode, turning your attention inward can increase distress rather than reduce it. People with a history of severe trauma sometimes find that body-focused practices bring up difficult material. If mindfulness makes things worse, that does not mean you are doing it wrong. It means it is not the right tool for you right now. There are other ways to manage distress.

✎

  • whether body-focused exercises feel safe or triggering for me
  • what time of day I find it easiest to be still
  • whether I prefer guided practice or doing it alone
  • what other approaches to calming down work better for me

Understanding Mania and Hypomania

What is happening when everything speeds up

You can read this if you or someone you care about is experiencing a period where everything feels faster, bigger, or more intense than usual. If this is happening to you, it is important to know: you matter, and this experience is treatable. What feels like your best self may be the early stages of something that can escalate. That is not a judgement. It is worth understanding.

What mania feels like from the inside

Early stages often feel genuinely good. Energy increases, sleep seems unnecessary, ideas come fast, confidence rises, and the world feels vivid and full of possibility. This is part of what makes mania difficult to recognise and difficult to accept help for. Why would you want to stop feeling this way?

As mania escalates, the experience can shift:

  • sleep drops to very little or none, without feeling tired
  • speech becomes rapid, pressured, hard to interrupt
  • thoughts race faster than you can follow them
  • decisions become impulsive – spending, plans, risks that would normally seem unwise
  • irritability or paranoia can replace the initial euphoria
  • beliefs about your abilities or significance can become very strong
  • in severe mania, psychotic experiences can develop

Why insight reduces

One of the hardest aspects of mania is that the part of the brain responsible for accurately assessing your own state is genuinely impaired. This is not stubbornness or denial. It is the condition itself. The brain is telling you that you are fine – better than fine – while the people around you can see that something has changed. This disconnect is one of the most common and most difficult features of mania.

What is happening in the brain

During mania, the brain's reward and arousal systems become overactive. Dopamine – a chemical your brain already produces – surges beyond its normal range. This drives the elevated mood, the increased energy, the reduced need for sleep, and the sensation that everything is significant and everything is possible. The regulatory systems that would normally bring this back into balance struggle to keep up.

What staff are watching for

You may feel that staff are being cautious or restrictive when you feel fine. It can help to understand what they are monitoring:

  • sleep patterns – reduced sleep is both a symptom and a trigger for escalation
  • speech rate and volume – pressure of speech is a clinical indicator
  • grandiosity – beliefs about abilities, plans, or significance that seem out of proportion
  • risk-taking – financial, sexual, or physical risks
  • irritability or agitation, especially if challenged

The comedown

What is rarely talked about openly is what happens after a manic episode. The crash can be severe – exhaustion, depression, shame about things said or done during the episode, embarrassment, and grief for the lost high. Some people describe it as the worst part of the whole cycle. If you are in this phase, it is worth knowing that this is a recognised part of the pattern, not a separate problem. It passes, though it does not feel that way while it is happening.

Why people stop medication

Many people with bipolar disorder stop their medication during a well period, or because they want to experience the highs again. This is one of the most common and most understandable patterns in bipolar management. Hypomania in particular can feel like the best version of yourself. Being told to medicate that away can feel like a loss, not a gain. If you feel conflicted about this, that is worth discussing honestly with your care team rather than stopping quietly.

What helps during a manic episode

  • protect sleep above everything else – sleep disruption is the single biggest escalation factor
  • reduce stimulation – noise, activity, and social contact can fuel the episode
  • a trusted person who can gently reality-check without being confrontational
  • routine and structure, even minimal
  • medication review – early intervention with medication can shorten an episode significantly
  • avoid major decisions – financial, relational, or professional decisions made during mania often look very different afterwards

Care Planning Tips

It can help to tell staff:

  • what my early warning signs of mania look like – what I notice, what others notice
  • what my sleep was like before this started
  • whether I recognise that things have changed, or whether I feel completely fine
  • what has helped in previous episodes and what has not

Understanding Delirium

When confusion comes on suddenly and is caused by a physical problem

You can read this if you or someone you care about has become suddenly confused, disorientated, or not themselves. If this has happened quickly – over hours or days rather than weeks – it may be delirium. Delirium is a medical condition, not a mental illness. It is caused by a physical problem affecting the brain, and it is usually reversible once the cause is treated.

What delirium is

Delirium is a sudden change in how the brain processes information. It affects attention, awareness, and thinking. The person may not know where they are, what day it is, or who the people around them are. It can come and go – someone may seem lucid one hour and very confused the next. This fluctuation is one of the hallmarks of delirium.

How it differs from dementia

Delirium comes on quickly – hours to days. Dementia develops gradually over months or years. Delirium fluctuates throughout the day. Dementia is more consistent. Delirium is usually reversible. Dementia is not. However, people with existing dementia are at higher risk of developing delirium, which can make diagnosis harder.

How it differs from psychosis

Both delirium and psychosis can involve seeing or hearing things that are not there. The difference is that in delirium, consciousness itself is affected – attention wanders, awareness dips, and the person may drift in and out of responsiveness. Delirium is caused by a physical problem, and treating that problem is the priority.

Common physical causes

  • infection – urinary tract infections (UTIs) are one of the most common causes, especially in older adults
  • dehydration or poor nutrition
  • constipation, especially when severe
  • medication changes – starting, stopping, or changing doses
  • pain, especially unmanaged pain
  • surgery or anaesthesia
  • alcohol withdrawal
  • organ failure – kidney, liver, or respiratory

What it can look like

  • confusion that comes and goes
  • not knowing where you are or what day it is
  • seeing or hearing things that are not there
  • agitation, especially at night (sometimes called sundowning)
  • drowsiness or reduced awareness
  • being unable to follow a conversation or stay focused
  • pulling at lines, trying to get out of bed, or becoming very distressed without clear reason

Why it can be frightening

For the person experiencing delirium, the confusion can be terrifying. The world may not make sense. Familiar people may not be recognised. Strange things may be seen or heard. For family and friends, watching someone become suddenly confused and unlike themselves is deeply distressing. It is worth knowing that the person often has little or no memory of the episode afterwards.

What helps

  • treating the underlying physical cause – this is the most important step
  • calm, quiet environment with good lighting
  • familiar faces and consistent staff where possible
  • reorientation – a clock, a calendar, photographs, gentle reminders of where they are
  • hydration and nutrition
  • keeping communication simple, clear, and calm
  • reducing unnecessary medication where safe to do so

What does not help

  • arguing with the confused person or trying to correct their beliefs forcefully
  • quizzing them repeatedly on the date, their name, or where they are
  • lots of unfamiliar people or frequent changes of environment
  • restraining someone who is agitated unless absolutely necessary for safety

Recovery

Most delirium resolves once the physical cause is treated. Recovery can take days to weeks. Some people, particularly older adults, may take longer to return fully to baseline. The person may have little or no memory of being delirious, which can itself be confusing and distressing when they are told about it afterwards.

✎

Care Planning Tips

If someone is recovering from delirium, or if you are supporting them, it can help to share:

  • what the person is normally like – their baseline
  • when the confusion started and what changed around that time
  • what medications they are taking
  • what helps them feel calm and oriented
  • whether they have had delirium before and what helped then

When Infection Affects the Mind

Understanding confusion, psychosis, or personality changes caused by physical illness

You can read this if you or someone you care about has experienced sudden mental health changes that may be linked to a physical illness or infection. If this is happening, it is important to know: these changes are usually treatable, and they are not a sign of a new or worsening mental illness.

UTIs and the mind

Urinary tract infections are one of the most common causes of sudden confusion, agitation, and personality changes – especially in older adults. A UTI can cause hallucinations, paranoia, aggression, disorientation, and behaviour that looks nothing like the person's usual self. In older adults, a UTI may produce no typical urinary symptoms at all. The first sign may be purely mental – sudden confusion, agitation, or withdrawal. This is widely documented but still frequently missed.

Other infections that can affect the brain

  • chest infections and pneumonia – especially in older adults or those with existing conditions
  • sepsis – a severe whole-body response to infection that can cause confusion, agitation, and altered consciousness
  • COVID-19 and post-viral syndromes – can cause cognitive changes, confusion, and psychiatric symptoms
  • encephalitis – inflammation of the brain itself, which can produce psychotic symptoms, confusion, seizures, and personality changes

Why this happens

When the body fights infection, the immune system releases inflammatory chemicals called cytokines. These can cross the blood-brain barrier and disrupt normal brain function. High temperature alone can cause confusion. The combination of inflammation, dehydration, pain, and immune response can temporarily alter how the brain processes information, leading to symptoms that look psychiatric but are actually medical.

How to tell the difference from a mental health crisis

The key differences are usually:

  • sudden onset – days or hours, not weeks
  • no previous psychiatric history, or symptoms that look very different from the person's usual pattern
  • physical symptoms present – fever, pain, reduced fluid intake, changes in urine
  • fluctuating consciousness – the person may drift in and out of awareness
  • confusion and disorientation alongside any hallucinations or paranoia

If someone with a known mental health condition suddenly becomes much more confused or disorientated than usual, infection should always be considered. Mental illness does not protect against physical illness, and physical illness can destabilise a previously stable mental health presentation.

Why this matters

The most important thing is that the infection is identified and treated. If the underlying cause is physical, treating only the behavioural symptoms with psychiatric medication will not solve the problem and may mask what is actually going on. Appropriate medical investigation – blood tests, urine samples, physical examination – should be the first step.

What helps

  • medical treatment of the infection – antibiotics, fluids, temperature management
  • reassurance and simple communication
  • familiar objects and people
  • hydration and nutrition
  • monitoring for deterioration
  • patience – recovery from the mental symptoms can lag behind recovery from the infection by days or even weeks

Recovery

Mental symptoms usually improve as the infection clears, though this can take longer than people expect. Some confusion or fatigue may persist for days to weeks after the infection itself has resolved. The person may have little memory of the episode. If full cognitive function does not return within a reasonable timeframe, further investigation may be needed.

✎

Care Planning Tips

If infection-related mental health changes are suspected, it can help to share:

  • when the changes started and how quickly they developed
  • what the person is normally like – their baseline personality and functioning
  • any physical symptoms – fever, pain, reduced fluid intake, changes in urine
  • any recent medication changes
  • whether they have had similar episodes before linked to physical illness

Understanding Catatonia

When the body freezes, slows, or moves in unusual ways

You can read this if you or someone you care about has experienced a state where the body seems to freeze, slow down dramatically, or move in unusual ways that do not seem voluntary. If this is happening, it is important to know: catatonia is a recognised neurological state. It is not a choice, and it is treatable.

What catatonia is

Catatonia is a condition where the brain's motor control systems become disrupted. It can happen alongside mental illness, physical illness, or both. The person is not being difficult, lazy, or uncooperative. Their brain's ability to initiate, control, or stop movement is genuinely impaired.

What it can look like

Catatonia exists on a spectrum. It can be subtle or severe.

  • not moving or speaking for prolonged periods
  • holding unusual postures without seeming to notice
  • waxy flexibility – limbs stay in whatever position staff place them in
  • repetitive movements or repeated words and phrases
  • refusal or inability to eat or drink
  • staring, unresponsiveness, or appearing completely withdrawn
  • in some forms, the opposite – extreme agitation, purposeless movement, or excitement

Awareness may be preserved even when movement is not. A person in a catatonic state may be able to hear and understand everything happening around them, even if they cannot respond. Staff should always speak respectfully and explain what is happening, regardless of whether the person appears to be aware.

Why it happens

Catatonia involves disruption to brain circuits that use GABA and dopamine – chemicals your brain already produces. It can be triggered by:

  • severe mental illness – particularly psychosis, severe depression, or bipolar disorder
  • medication reactions – especially starting or stopping certain medications
  • physical illness – infections, autoimmune conditions, metabolic problems
  • extreme stress or psychological overwhelm
  • stopping benzodiazepines suddenly

Why early recognition matters

Untreated catatonia can become a medical emergency. Prolonged immobility increases the risk of dehydration, blood clots, pressure sores, and muscle breakdown. The longer it continues, the harder it can be to treat. Early recognition and treatment make a significant difference to outcomes.

Treatment

Catatonia usually responds well to treatment once it is correctly identified.

  • benzodiazepines (such as lorazepam) – often the first-line treatment and frequently very effective
  • ECT (electroconvulsive therapy) – in severe or treatment-resistant cases, ECT is highly effective for catatonia
  • importantly, some antipsychotic medications can make catatonia worse – this is a critical clinical consideration

If someone who is acutely psychotic suddenly becomes immobile, mute, or rigid, this should be assessed as possible catatonia rather than assumed to be part of the psychosis. The treatment may need to change.

Recovery

Many people recover fully with appropriate treatment. Some people experience recurrent episodes. If you have had catatonia before, it is important that this is clearly documented in your notes so that staff can recognise it quickly if it happens again.

✎

Care Planning Tips

If catatonia is part of your history, or if you are supporting someone with catatonia:

  • document previous episodes clearly – what it looked like, what helped, what made it worse
  • note which medications were being taken when it started
  • specify whether benzodiazepines helped in the past
  • record any known triggers
  • remind staff: the person may be aware even when they cannot respond – always explain what you are doing

Stopping Substances and Mental Health

Understanding brain changes after regular substance use – without blame

You can read this if you are curious about why mental health symptoms can sometimes worsen after stopping substances, even when stopping felt like the right thing to do. If this has been part of your experience, it is important to know: this is not a failure of willpower, character, or motivation. It reflects how the brain adapts to repeated chemical exposure and how slowly it can recalibrate afterwards.

Using substances does not mean there is something wrong with you.

Your brain already uses powerful chemical systems

Your brain relies on natural chemical messengers to regulate mood, motivation, arousal, sleep, stress, and emotional responses. Some of the key systems involved include:

  • Dopamine – involved in motivation, reward, pleasure, confidence, and goal-directed behaviour
  • Noradrenaline – involved in alertness, energy, focus, vigilance, and threat detection
  • Serotonin – involved in mood stability, emotional processing, impulse control, and sleep
  • Endocannabinoids – involved in emotional regulation, stress buffering, sleep, pain perception, and memory
  • Glutamate – the brain's main excitatory chemical, involved in learning and signal transmission
  • GABA – the brain's main calming chemical, involved in anxiety control, inhibition, and rest
  • Cortisol – part of the body's stress response system

Everyone has these systems. They are normal, necessary, and active all the time.

What regular substance use does in the brain

Substances do not introduce foreign systems. They intensely stimulate or suppress systems your brain already has, often in ways that override normal timing and regulation.

Stimulants (cocaine, crack cocaine, amphetamines)

Stimulants strongly increase dopamine and noradrenaline. With repeated use, the brain adapts by reducing dopamine receptor sensitivity, reducing baseline dopamine production, and shifting alertness and motivation systems to expect external stimulation. Over time, normal activity can feel flat or effortful without the substance.

Cannabis

Cannabis acts primarily on CB1 cannabinoid receptors, which normally help regulate emotional reactivity, stress responses, dopamine release, sleep-wake cycles, and memory processing. With repeated use, CB1 receptors become less responsive, dopamine signalling becomes less tightly regulated, and the brain's ability to dampen stress and emotional arousal weakens.

Alcohol

Alcohol amplifies GABA (calming) and suppresses glutamate (excitatory). With regular use, the brain reduces its own GABA activity and glutamate systems become more excitable to compensate. This leaves the brain prone to over-activation when alcohol is stopped.

Opiates (heroin, morphine, codeine, oxycodone)

Opiates activate the brain's natural opioid receptors, which regulate pain relief, emotional soothing, and reward and safety signalling. With repeated exposure, the brain produces fewer natural opioids, emotional and physical pain thresholds lower, and stress systems become more reactive.

Sedatives (benzodiazepines, z-drugs)

These act directly on GABA receptors. Over time, GABA receptors down-regulate and the brain becomes dependent on external calming input. This makes sudden stopping particularly destabilising.

Neuroadaptation: the brain adjusting to survive

Across substances, the same principle applies. The brain adapts to repeated chemical pressure. This process is called neuroadaptation. The brain may reduce receptor sensitivity, alter production of its own chemicals, shift stress thresholds, and change how alertness and sleep are regulated.

This is not damage. It is the brain trying to maintain stability in difficult conditions.

Why stopping can feel worse before it feels better

When substances are stopped suddenly, the brain does not instantly reset. Systems that have been turned down remain quiet, while systems that were compensating stay switched on. During this period:

  • dopamine signalling can become unstable rather than smoothly regulated
  • noradrenaline-driven alertness remains high
  • inhibitory systems (GABA, endocannabinoids) recover more slowly
  • sleep regulation is disrupted
  • cortisol (stress hormone) may remain elevated

This creates a state of loss of regulation, not absence of chemicals.

Hyperarousal and mania-like states

Without adequate inhibitory control, some people experience hyperarousal, which can include:

  • reduced need for sleep or inability to sleep
  • racing or pressured thoughts
  • increased energy, restlessness, or agitation
  • heightened threat perception
  • emotional intensity or irritability
  • grandiosity or overconfidence
  • paranoid or suspicious thinking

This state can closely resemble mania or acute psychosis, even in people with no previous history of these conditions. Importantly, this may occur after stopping substances, not during use.

This is not the brain over-producing one chemical

While cortisol and dopamine levels are often elevated in this phase, the core problem is not simply excess of one substance. What is happening is disrupted timing between systems, weakened braking mechanisms, stress circuits remaining switched on, and insufficient integration between emotional and rational processes. The brain is temporarily out of sync, not broken.

Why sleep is so important

Sleep is one of the brain's primary tools for recalibration. Without sleep, dopamine regulation worsens, stress hormones remain elevated, emotional control weakens, and psychotic or manic-like symptoms intensify. Supporting sleep is often one of the most important aspects of recovery during this phase.

Why medication may be offered

During this vulnerable period, medication may be offered to reduce excessive dopamine or noradrenaline activity, strengthen inhibitory systems, support sleep, and reduce distress while neuroadaptation reverses. This is not about punishment or labelling. It is about supporting the brain while it recalibrates.

What this experience does not mean

It does not mean you caused permanent brain damage, or that you were wrong to try to stop, or that your symptoms are exaggerated, or that you now have a lifelong diagnosis. The experiences are real, physical, and biologically driven.

A note on blame and context

Many people use substances to cope with trauma, poverty, loss, exclusion, untreated mental illness, or chronic stress. The brain adapts to help people survive these conditions. Problems after stopping are a predictable biological consequence, not a moral failing.

A final reassurance

If your mental health became more unstable after stopping substances, you did not fail. Your brain was adjusting to a sudden change in how its chemical systems had been operating. With time, safety, sleep, and support, these systems can stabilise again.

Stopping substances is still a positive step. Sometimes the brain just needs help to catch up.

  • whether symptoms worsened after stopping rather than during use
  • how your sleep has been affected
  • whether symptoms feel driven by anxiety, energy, or alertness
  • what you are most worried these symptoms might mean
